# Independence and Conflicts of Interest in the Quality Network

*The firewall between consulting and review.*

## About this course

**Who it is for.** This course is written for ICoBC Lead Reviewers (N2) and ICoBC Accredited Partners (N3). It has no prerequisites.

**Where it sits.** The course supports the ICoBC Credentialing Skills Framework (ICSF), skill QUAL "Quality assurance and accreditation" (VRITE pillar: Rigor), level L3 "Enable", stage K "Knowledge". At this level the skill is: runs self-assessment against a quality framework, prepares for external review and implements improvements. The Knowledge stage means that you understand the concepts, rules and standards, and the reasons behind them. You can explain and recognise.

**Learning outcomes.** After this course you can:

1. Explain the separation between consulting and review in the ICoBC model.
2. Identify conflicts of interest in realistic situations.
3. Apply the disclosure and recusal procedure.

**Workload.** About 1.5 hours of self-paced learning. Live or workshop elements are described in facilitator notes for the people who run them.

**Assessment.** A scenario quiz in the section "Assessment and badge". Full marks are required. You can retake the quiz as often as you need.

**Badge.** "Independence and Conflicts of Interest in the Quality Network (Knowledge, L3)", alignment code ICSF-QUAL-L3-K. The badge evidence is the passed quiz.

**Quality focus.** The trustworthiness of the Seal itself. Every review decision supports the Global Quality Seal only if people can rely on the independence of the people who made it.

## Why independence matters

### The two roles in the network

The ICoBC quality network has two kinds of work that must stay apart.

**Consulting** helps an organisation get ready. An ICoBC Accredited Partner (N3) may advise an education provider or an employer on how to meet a quality framework. The partner runs a self-assessment with the client, suggests improvements and helps to prepare for external review. This is the work described by ICSF skill QUAL at level L3.

**Review** judges whether the organisation meets the framework. An ICoBC Lead Reviewer (N2) examines the evidence and contributes to a recommendation. The reviewer does not coach the client on how to pass.

Both roles are useful. A client benefits from good advice, and the network benefits from sound review. The ICoBC model asks only that one person or one organisation does not do both for the same client. This separation is often called the **firewall** between consulting and review.

### Why the firewall exists

A seal is a promise. When a provider or employer shows the Global Quality Seal, other people rely on it without repeating the review themselves. Learners, employers and associations trust the Seal because they believe the judgement behind it was independent.

If the same person coached an organisation and then judged it, the judgement would be a review of their own advice. Even an honest person would find it hard to be critical. Other people would also have reason to doubt the result, even if no one ever did anything wrong. The ICoBC model therefore protects two things:

- **Actual independence:** the judgement is free from influence.
- **Perceived independence:** a reasonable outsider has no cause to think it is not.

Both matter. A review can be fair and still lose trust if it looks compromised. Because the Seal depends on trust, a visible firewall protects every holder of the Seal, including those who had no connection with the conflict.

### Independence, impartiality and objectivity

Three words appear often in quality work, and they are related.

- **Independence** describes the relationships around you: no financial, personal or organisational ties that could steer a judgement.
- **Impartiality** describes your attitude: you give no side an advantage.
- **Objectivity** describes your method: you judge from evidence and criteria.

Independence supports the other two. When there are no ties, impartiality and objectivity are easier to keep and easier to show. When ties exist, the person must either remove them or step away from the decision.

General rules of independence are widely used in conformity assessment and accreditation. ICoBC bases its approach on the same ideas. Details such as formal cooling-off periods or thresholds are set by ICoBC governance documents, not by this course. <!-- VERIFY: Check current ICoBC policy on cooling-off periods and any thresholds before publishing. -->

**Worked example: The same firm on both sides**

Dana is an ICoBC Accredited Partner (N3). Her firm helps a regional training provider, Northfield Academy, to run a self-assessment and fix gaps in its quality processes. Six months later Northfield applies for external review. The review panel is short of people and asks whether Dana could join it, because she knows the provider well.

Dana's knowledge is real, and that is the problem. She would be judging processes that she helped to design. The firewall rule says that she must not review a client she has advised. The panel must find another reviewer. Dana may still tell the panel that she has a conflict, and that disclosure helps the network. She does not give the panel any advice about how to judge Northfield.

Note what Dana does not need to do. She does not need to stop being a consultant, and she does not need to leave the network. She declines this one review.

**Practice activity: Name the role**

Look at the diagram again and cover the labels with your hand if you can. For each statement, decide whether it describes consulting, review, or a breach of the firewall:

1. "I helped the client write its improvement plan."
2. "I read the evidence and wrote my findings."
3. "I told the client what the panel would want to see, then sat on the panel."

Write your answers down, then check them: statement 1 is consulting, statement 2 is review, and statement 3 is a breach of the firewall because one person is on both sides.

## Typical conflicts

### What a conflict of interest is

A **conflict of interest** exists when a person has a personal, financial, professional or organisational interest that could influence, or could reasonably appear to influence, their judgement in a role of trust. A conflict is a situation, not an accusation. Many people in the network hold several roles and will meet conflicts. The matter becomes a problem only when the situation is hidden or ignored.

Three types help you to recognise conflicts quickly.

- **Actual conflict:** the interest exists now and relates directly to the decision.
- **Potential conflict:** the interest could become relevant, for example if circumstances change.
- **Apparent conflict:** a reasonable outsider would suspect influence, even if there is none.

The ICoBC approach treats all three seriously. You disclose potential and apparent conflicts, and the procedure in module 3 decides what happens next.

### Five typical sources

**1. Prior consulting.** You, or your organisation, advised the client on the matter now under review. This is the central case of the firewall. It also applies to colleagues in the same firm if the firm benefits from the client relationship.

**2. Financial interest.** You hold shares in the client, receive fees from it, or expect future work from it. Expected work counts, because the hope of a contract can shape a judgement.

**3. Close personal relationship.** The client's leader is a relative, a close friend or a former partner. Equally, an open dispute with that person is a conflict. Strong dislike and strong liking both affect judgement.

**4. Competing interest.** You work for, or advise, a competitor of the client. You could gain from a negative result, or you could see commercially sensitive information that you should not use.

**5. Gifts and hospitality.** The client offers a gift, a trip or a generous meal around the time of a review. Small courtesies such as a coffee during a site visit are normal. Anything of real value, or anything that could look like thanks in advance, needs disclosure. Policies on value limits come from ICoBC governance documents. <!-- VERIFY: Check current ICoBC gifts and hospitality policy, including any monetary threshold, before publishing. -->

### Employment and time

Conflicts can come from the past and from the future. A person who worked for the client two years ago may still hold loyalties or knowledge that matter. A person who is negotiating to join the client has a strong interest in how the review turns out. Ask both questions: "What ties did I have?" and "What ties could I soon have?"

Another common case involves organisations that have several arms. A partner's training department may sell courses to the client while its quality team carries out an assessment. The people may differ, but the organisation benefits from both. This is an organisational conflict, and the firewall must work at the organisational level as well as the personal one.

### Not every tie is a conflict

Being part of the same professional network, attending the same conference or having reviewed other, unrelated clients does not create a conflict. The test is whether the tie could influence, or could reasonably appear to influence, this particular judgement. When you are unsure, disclose and let the procedure decide. Silence is the one response that always creates a problem.

**Worked example: The offer of a future contract**

Marek is an ICoBC Lead Reviewer (N2) assigned to review Riverside Employers Forum, an association of companies that offers credentials to its members. During the preparation call, Riverside's director mentions that the forum is planning a large quality improvement programme next year and is looking for a trusted adviser. She says that Marek's name came up.

Marek has no financial tie yet and no relationship with the director. But he now has a possible future interest in the client, which makes this a potential conflict, and one that a reasonable outsider might see as apparent. Marek does not need to decide alone whether he is still independent. He tells the review coordinator about the remark, in writing, and follows the procedure. He also does not respond to the idea of future work while the review is open.

**Practice activity: Sort the situations**

Place each situation under one of these headings: *conflict*, *possible conflict, disclose*, or *no conflict*. Use the five sources above.

1. A Lead Reviewer once spoke on the same conference panel as the client's quality manager.
2. A Lead Reviewer's sister is the head of the client's quality unit.
3. A Lead Reviewer's employer is about to bid for consulting work with the client.
4. The client sends a bunch of flowers to the review team after the site visit.

Check yourself: situation 1 is no conflict. Situation 2 is a conflict. Situation 3 is a possible conflict that must be disclosed. Situation 4 is a possible conflict, so disclose it and ask for guidance, because the value and timing matter.

## The procedure

### Three steps: disclose, assess, act

When you notice a possible conflict, follow a short and repeatable procedure. The general rules are the same in most quality systems, and ICoBC applies them to Lead Reviewers and Accredited Partners.

1. **Disclose.** Tell the review coordinator, in writing and as early as possible, what the tie is. Describe the facts. Do not argue that it is harmless.
2. **Assess.** A person other than you decides whether the conflict is serious and what to do. You do not judge your own case.
3. **Act.** The decision is recorded. If the conflict is serious, you **recuse** yourself, which means that you step out of the review and take no part in the findings or the decision. If it is minor, the decision may allow you to continue with named safeguards.

Disclosure is a duty. It is also a protection for you, because a recorded disclosure shows that you acted in good faith.

### What a good disclosure contains

A disclosure is short and factual. It should state:

- who you are and the role you hold in this review or engagement;
- the client or case concerned;
- the nature of the tie, such as consulting work, a family link or a financial interest;
- when the tie started, and whether it has ended;
- what you have already done, for example whether you have seen documents or taken part in a discussion.

You do not need to prove that you would stay fair. You also do not need to explain at length why you think the tie is harmless. Your own view of your fairness is not the test, because the question is how the tie looks to others and what it could do.

Make the disclosure early. A tie that is raised before the review begins is easy to handle: another reviewer takes the place. A tie that is raised halfway through, or after the report, can invalidate work already done and damage trust in the result.

### What recusal means in practice

When you recuse yourself, you step out completely. In practice:

- you stop reading and receiving documents for that review;
- you take no part in meetings, discussions or decisions about it;
- you do not talk to the other team members or to the client about the case;
- you hand over any material you have, as the coordinator instructs;
- you do not advise anyone on the outcome.

Recusal is neutral. It is not a penalty and it is not an admission of wrongdoing. It is the normal result of an honest disclosure when a conflict is serious.

Where a conflict is minor, the coordinator may decide that you continue with safeguards. A safeguard could be a second reviewer who checks your part of the work, or a limit on what you review. The safeguards are written down so that others can see them. The decision belongs to the coordinator, not to you.

### Records, ongoing duties and unclear cases

Keep a copy of your disclosure and the written decision. If your circumstances change during the review, disclose again. A new job offer, a new client relationship or a change in your organisation can all create a conflict that did not exist at the start.

If you are unsure whether something counts, disclose. The cost of a disclosure that turns out to be unnecessary is small. The cost of a conflict that comes to light later is large, both for the people concerned and for the trust in the Seal.

**Worked example: A disclosure on day two**

Amara is an ICoBC Lead Reviewer (N2). She is part of a three-person team reviewing Lakeshore College. On the first evening, while reading the self-assessment, she notices that the improvement plan was written with help from Tomas, who is a colleague at her own firm. She did not know that her firm had worked with the college.

Amara has not discussed the college with Tomas, and she has not seen any of his files. Even so, her firm has an interest in how the college is judged. She follows the procedure. She writes to the review coordinator the same evening and states the facts: her firm's earlier consulting, her lack of contact with Tomas about this case, and the documents she has read so far. The coordinator decides that the conflict is serious because it is organisational and directly tied to the plan under review. Amara recuses herself. She hands over her notes, and the coordinator appoints a replacement. The decision is recorded.

Amara acted as soon as she saw the issue and gave clear facts. The review team lost one day, and the Seal lost nothing.

**Practice activity: Write a disclosure**

Imagine this situation. You are an Accredited Partner (N3). You have been invited to join a panel reviewing a provider called Harbour Institute. You realise that your cousin teaches there part-time and may be affected by the findings.

Write a disclosure of three to five sentences for the review coordinator. Include your role, the case, the nature of the tie and what you have already done. Then check your text against the list in "What a good disclosure contains". A good answer is short, factual and early, and it does not argue that the tie is harmless.

**Question 1.** Which description matches the ICoBC model?

The firewall separates consulting from review for the same client. Different people in the same organisation do not solve the problem, and client consent does not remove it, because the trust in the Seal belongs to everyone who relies on it.

The Seal is a shared signal of trust. A compromised review damages the confidence that all holders depend on, so a visible firewall protects them all.

A close personal relationship is a typical source of conflict. The reviewer's view of their own fairness does not settle the matter. Disclosure lets another person decide.

A gift of real value around a review could look like thanks in advance and must be disclosed. The coordinator decides how to handle it, and the safest response is to decline.

A competing interest is a typical source of conflict. The reviewer discloses as soon as they notice it. Someone else then assesses the seriousness and decides on the action.

The decision belongs to someone other than the person with the conflict. It is recorded so that the reasons are visible later.
